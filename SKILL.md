# Professional Writing

Produce writing that leads a defined audience from a real need to a well-supported conclusion. Treat supplied material as evidence, not as the document structure.

## Establish the brief

Infer these items from the request and source material before drafting. Ask only when a missing choice would materially change the document:

- deliverable type and target audience;
- desired reader understanding, belief, decision, or action;
- one central claim and the most important conclusion;
- available evidence and the boundary it supports;
- length, format, venue, and tone constraints.

Express the argument in one sentence before writing substantial prose:

> Because A matters and existing approaches are limited by B, the work introduces C through mechanism D, supported by evidence E, with implication F.

If this chain is not coherent, repair it before polishing sentences.

## Build the argument

Organize for the reader's understanding, not for the chronology of the work:

1. Establish the need or decision context.
2. Make the difficulty concrete through causes, couplings, conflicts, or constraints.
3. Group existing approaches by idea or function and identify the unresolved gap.
4. Derive design requirements from that gap before introducing the solution.
5. Explain the solution's mechanism and how its parts address those requirements.
6. Match each major claim to theory, data, literature, cases, or analysis.
7. Interpret what the evidence means and why it matters.

Use one main argumentative job per section and per paragraph. A substantive paragraph normally follows:

> claim or topic sentence -> explanation -> evidence -> implication -> transition

Do not force this sequence when a shorter paragraph is clearer.

## Write with evidence discipline

- Calibrate claim strength to the evidence. Do not invent facts, citations, data, or consensus.
- Distinguish background, assumptions, conditions, constraints, objectives, methods, metrics, and evidence.
- Prefer mechanisms and specific results to empty adjectives such as “advanced,” “significant,” or “excellent.”
- Emphasize the strongest supported contribution, but do not hide facts that would change the conclusion.
- When a component metric is weaker, handle it briefly and return to the task-level criterion only when that criterion is genuinely supported.
- Treat examples as illustrations, not population-level evidence.
- If evidence is missing, identify the gap outside the polished draft or use an explicit placeholder when the user requests a draft with placeholders.

For quantitative comparisons, figures, tables, or experimental evidence, read [references/evidence-and-visuals.md](references/evidence-and-visuals.md).

## Maintain professional style

- Define unfamiliar or newly coined terms; prefer established terminology when it is adequate.
- Keep terminology stable across headings, prose, equations, figures, and conclusions.
- Remove process narration, code commentary, revision reports, and other author-workflow details unless the genre explicitly requires them.
- Avoid defensive disclaimers such as “we do not claim” or “this does not guarantee.” State the supported scope positively and directly.
- Avoid absolute superiority, causality, novelty, or universality claims unless the evidence establishes them.
- Remove repeated background, parameter lists, conditions, and numerical recitations.
- Let tables carry exact values, figures carry patterns, and prose carry interpretation.

Do not impose academic-paper conventions on every document. For genre-specific argument shapes, read [references/deliverable-modes.md](references/deliverable-modes.md) and use only the relevant section.

## Revise in layers

Revise in this order:

1. central claim and audience outcome;
2. argument chain and section functions;
3. claim–evidence alignment;
4. paragraph logic and transitions;
5. terminology, concision, and tone;
6. figures, tables, citations, cross-references, and final layout.

When reviewing or finalizing a substantial document, read [references/review-checklist.md](references/review-checklist.md). Report important evidence gaps separately from the revised prose so the document does not read like a review memo.
