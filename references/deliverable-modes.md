# Deliverable modes

Use only the section matching the requested document. These are default argument shapes, not mandatory templates.

## Academic paper or thesis

Reader questions:

- What scholarly or technical problem is being addressed?
- Why is it difficult and what remains unresolved?
- What is new in the method, theory, evidence, or understanding?
- Do the analysis and experiments support the contribution?

Typical argument shape:

> importance -> concrete challenge -> literature organized by approach -> gap -> contribution -> formulation -> method and mechanism -> evidence -> implications

Apply these rules:

- Integrate related work with the gap argument unless the venue or volume of literature warrants a separate section.
- Cite the original source for models and algorithms and direct sources for factual claims.
- Introduce method motivation and design requirements before equations or architecture details.
- State theoretical assumptions inside definitions, propositions, or analysis rather than as defensive closing disclaimers.
- Separate external baseline comparison from internal ablation: the former establishes relative performance; the latter explains contribution and necessity.
- Make conclusions answer the opening problem and connect results back to the proposed mechanism.

## Technical, research, or consulting report

Reader questions:

- What is the present state and why does it matter?
- What causes the problem?
- What options exist and how should they be judged?
- What action is recommended, with what cost and risk?

Typical argument shape:

> executive conclusion -> context -> observed facts -> causal analysis -> options and criteria -> recommendation -> implementation path

Separate facts, analysis, recommendations, and decisions. Engineering and operational details are appropriate when they help execution, but do not mix work-log narration with conclusions.

## Proposal or research plan

Reader questions:

- Why is investment justified now?
- What specific objective will be achieved?
- Why is the approach feasible and distinctive?
- What outputs, impacts, milestones, and risks are expected?

Typical argument shape:

> need and opportunity -> gap -> objectives -> approach -> novelty -> feasibility -> work plan -> outputs and impact -> risk controls

Distinguish existing evidence from proposed work. Present expected results as targets or hypotheses, never as completed achievements.

## Literature review

Reader questions:

- What core problems organize the field?
- What major approaches or schools of thought exist?
- What has each approach resolved, and under what assumptions?
- Where are the disagreements, limitations, and open questions?

Organize by problem, mechanism, method family, or historical transition—not by one-paper-at-a-time summaries. Each group should end with a synthesis that advances the review's central interpretation.

## Results analysis or evaluation

Reader questions:

- What changed, compared with what, and by how much?
- Is the change practically or statistically meaningful?
- What mechanism plausibly explains it?
- Does the evidence support the requested decision or claim?

Use the sequence:

> evaluation purpose -> protocol and comparison basis -> principal pattern -> quantitative evidence -> mechanism or interpretation -> decision-level implication

Do not merely narrate figures or repeat every number. Combine related figures when they support one conclusion.

## Achievement summary

Reader questions:

- What original objective was pursued?
- What verifiable capability, result, or output was created?
- What value or impact followed?
- What is the most important next step?

Distinguish outcomes from effort. Activities, meetings, code volume, and elapsed time are not achievements unless they establish a relevant capability or result.
